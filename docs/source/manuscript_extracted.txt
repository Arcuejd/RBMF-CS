[0011] Abstract
[0012] Polar ocean sensing requires reliable reconstruction of large-scale environmental fields from a limited number of observations, yet practical deployments are simultaneously affected by heterogeneous data fidelity, non-Gaussian disturbances, sea-ice conditions, energy limitations, and communication requirements. Existing sparse sensor placement approaches mainly optimize deterministic reconstruction error, information content, or deployment cost, and therefore provide limited support for uncertainty-aware decision making under coupled engineering constraints. This study proposes a unified framework that couples robust Bayesian multi-fidelity compressed sensing (RBMF-CS) with multi-constrained effective-information-ratio (MC-EIR) sensor deployment. In the reconstruction layer, high- and low-fidelity data are linked through a probabilistic discrepancy model, while hierarchical sparse priors and a heavy-tailed observation model jointly infer latent sparse coefficients, fidelity bias, noise scale, and posterior state uncertainty. The resulting posterior mean provides the reconstructed ocean field, whereas the posterior variance supplies a spatial uncertainty map for deployment decisions. In the deployment layer, the marginal value of a candidate node is defined by an effective information gain that combines entropy gain, posterior uncertainty reduction, and information redundancy. Sea-ice concentration, the maximum number of nodes, total energy consumption, and communication connectivity are treated as hard feasibility constraints; maintenance cost and node failure risk are incorporated as soft penalties. The two layers form a closed reconstruction–deployment loop in which sensing locations are selected according to the amount of reliable new information obtained per unit system cost. A comprehensive evaluation protocol is designed to assess reconstruction accuracy, probabilistic calibration, robustness to distribution shift and abnormal observations, deployment feasibility, energy and communication cost, and ablation effects. The proposed formulation extends entropy-weighted sparse placement from deterministic information maximization to uncertainty-aware, multi-fidelity, engineering-feasible sensing optimization for polar marine monitoring.

[0015] 1. Introduction
[0016] Large-scale monitoring of polar oceans is scientifically important but operationally difficult. Dense in-situ observation networks are expensive to deploy and maintain, and the feasible locations of sensing nodes vary with sea-ice conditions, accessibility, power availability, and communication infrastructure. Sparse sensing therefore offers an attractive alternative: a limited number of strategically selected measurements can be used to reconstruct a high-dimensional environmental field when the field possesses low-dimensional or sparse structure.
[0017] The methodological basis of this study is the earlier entropy-weighted sparse deployment framework for marine sensing. That work used temporal entropy weighting for sample screening and an entropy-weighted QR pivoting strategy for spatial sensor selection, thereby coordinating information acquisition, reconstruction error, and deployment cost. It further introduced sea-ice concentration into Arctic sensor placement so that selected nodes tended to move toward lower-ice, more navigable regions. These results established the feasibility of combining data-driven spatial structure with environmental deployment constraints [1].
[0018] However, two gaps remain if sparse polar sensing is considered as a system-level decision problem rather than an offline reconstruction problem. First, polar monitoring commonly relies on heterogeneous sources such as reanalysis or numerical-model products, remote sensing, and sparse high-quality observations. These sources differ in fidelity, resolution, bias, and noise characteristics. Deterministic reconstruction methods usually return a point estimate but do not quantify how strongly the estimate should be trusted at each location. Second, a high-information location is not necessarily deployable. A candidate sensor may violate sea-ice limits, exhaust the energy budget, or break communication connectivity even when its entropy or reconstruction benefit is large.
[0019] A recent review of IoT sensor-network evaluation provides a useful system-level perspective: algorithmic performance should be distinguished from deployment feasibility, and evaluation should jointly consider resource budgets, communication overhead, environmental non-stationarity, and downstream operational consequences [2]. Although that review focuses on adversarial detection rather than ocean reconstruction, its evidence-oriented taxonomy and deployment-aware evaluation philosophy motivate the present formulation. In particular, the distinction between predictive uncertainty, reconstruction evidence, runtime context, and multi-strategy fusion provides a useful organizational lens for reviewing sparse marine sensing methods.
[0020] Accordingly, this paper reframes sparse sensor placement as a coupled probabilistic reconstruction and deployment decision problem. The first objective is to infer a robust posterior distribution of the target ocean field from heterogeneous-fidelity data and sparse measurements. The second objective is to select a feasible sensor set that maximizes the reliable new information obtained per unit system cost. Posterior uncertainty from the reconstruction model is therefore not only an output metric; it becomes an explicit input to the next deployment decision.
[0021] The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
[0022] A robust Bayesian multi-fidelity compressed sensing model is formulated to jointly infer sparse latent coefficients, fidelity discrepancy, observation noise, and posterior uncertainty. Heavy-tailed likelihood modeling is introduced to reduce sensitivity to non-Gaussian noise and outliers.
[0023] An effective information gain is defined by combining entropy gain, posterior uncertainty reduction, and redundancy suppression. On this basis, an effective information ratio quantifies the marginal value of a candidate sensor relative to its deployment and operational burden.
[0024] A multi-constrained sensor placement formulation explicitly separates hard feasibility constraints from soft operational penalties. Sea-ice concentration, node number, energy, and communication connectivity define the feasible deployment domain; maintenance cost and failure risk influence preference within that domain.
[0025] A closed reconstruction–deployment algorithm is designed so that posterior uncertainty is iteratively converted into sensing actions. The evaluation protocol jointly examines reconstruction accuracy, uncertainty calibration, robustness, deployment cost, connectivity, and constraint ablations rather than relying on a single offline error metric.
[0026] The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews sparse sensor placement and reconstruction methods using an evidence-oriented six-family structure. Section 3 formulates the polar sensing problem and the multi-fidelity observation model. Section 4 presents the RBMF-CS reconstruction method. Section 5 develops the MC-EIR deployment formulation and constrained optimization algorithm. Section 6 describes the reconstruction–deployment closed loop and evaluation protocol. Section 7 reports and discusses the experimental results. Section 8 concludes the paper and outlines future work.
[0027] 2. Related Work
[0028] Rather than organizing the literature only by algorithm names, this section follows an evidence-oriented structure adapted from the six-family taxonomy in [2]. The categories are translated to the marine sensing problem and used to clarify what information each method exploits, what it contributes to deployment, and where its limitations arise.
[0029] 2.1 Data- and Input-Consistency Methods
[0030] These methods emphasize quality control before reconstruction, including denoising, physical-range checks, cross-source consistency, and abnormal-value suppression. Their main advantage is low computational cost and direct physical interpretability. However, fixed preprocessing may suppress meaningful extremes or fail when environmental regimes shift. In the proposed framework, this family motivates robust observation likelihoods rather than a rigid preprocessing-only solution.
[0031] 2.2 Feature-Statistics and Subspace Methods
[0032] POD/PCA, D-optimal design, QR pivoting, entropy weighting, and related subspace methods select sensors from dominant spatial modes or statistical structure. The previous entropy-weighted QR approach belongs primarily to this family and demonstrated that information content, reconstruction error, and deployment cost can be considered jointly [1]. These methods are efficient and interpretable, but most produce deterministic selections and do not directly quantify posterior state uncertainty.
[0033] 2.3 Predictive-Uncertainty Methods
[0034] Bayesian compressed sensing, sparse Bayesian learning, Gaussian-process models, and probabilistic state estimators quantify uncertainty rather than returning only a point reconstruction. This capability is essential when observations are sparse or heterogeneous. Nevertheless, standard Bayesian formulations may assume a single fidelity level and light-tailed Gaussian noise, which can be inadequate for polar observations containing source-dependent bias and abnormal disturbances.
[0035] 2.4 Model-Reconstruction Methods
[0036] Compressed sensing, low-rank reconstruction, autoencoders, and generative models recover high-dimensional fields from partial measurements. Their deployment value depends on whether reconstruction remains stable under sparse sampling, environmental shift, and data mismatch. Reconstruction accuracy alone is insufficient when the model cannot indicate where its estimates are unreliable.
[0037] 2.5 Runtime-Context and Constraint-Aware Methods
[0038] Context-aware deployment incorporates environmental and operational information that is external to the reconstruction model, such as sea-ice concentration, accessibility, energy state, communication range, or route constraints. The Arctic extension in [1] demonstrated the practical effect of sea-ice concentration on feasible sensor placement. The present work extends runtime context from a single environmental factor to a coupled feasible domain with ice, node-budget, energy, and connectivity constraints.
[0039] 2.6 Multi-Strategy Fusion Methods
[0040] Fusion methods combine complementary evidence sources rather than relying on a single score. For polar sensing, useful evidence includes entropy, reconstruction uncertainty, spatial redundancy, environmental feasibility, and system cost. The central hypothesis of this paper is that a candidate node should be selected only when it contributes reliable new information while remaining feasible under engineering constraints.
[0041] 2.7 Research Gap and Positioning
[0042] The literature therefore reveals a missing connection between probabilistic reconstruction and deployment optimization. Existing entropy- or QR-based placement methods identify informative locations but do not explicitly exploit posterior uncertainty from multi-fidelity reconstruction. Conversely, Bayesian reconstruction methods can quantify uncertainty but often stop at state estimation rather than converting uncertainty into constrained sensing actions. This paper addresses that gap by coupling RBMF-CS and MC-EIR in a single decision loop.
[0043] 3. Problem Formulation and System Model
[0044] 3.1 Polar Ocean Field and Sparse Observations
[0045] Let x_H ∈ R^N denote the target high-fidelity ocean state over N candidate spatial locations. A low-dimensional representation is assumed:
[0046] x_H = Ψ α,
[0047] where Ψ ∈ R^(N×K) is a spatial basis and α ∈ R^K is a sparse or compressible coefficient vector. For a selected node set J, |J| = p, the sparse observation model is
[0048] y_J = C_J x_H + ε = C_J Ψ α + ε,
[0049] where C_J is the sampling matrix and ε represents observation error.
[0050] 3.2 Multi-Fidelity Relationship
[0051] Let x_L denote a lower-fidelity field with broader coverage but lower reliability. A parsimonious autoregressive discrepancy model is adopted as the baseline formulation:
[0052] x_H = ρ x_L + δ,
[0053] where ρ is a fidelity-coupling coefficient and δ is a discrepancy field. The formulation can be extended to multiple low-fidelity sources by replacing ρx_L with a weighted latent fusion term. The key requirement is that fidelity mismatch is inferred rather than treated as a fixed preprocessing correction.
[0054] 3.3 Hard Feasibility Constraints
[0055] A deployment set is considered feasible only if it satisfies four hard constraints:
[0056] ζ_j ≤ I_c,  ∀ j ∈ J,
[0057] |J| ≤ p_max,
[0058] E(J) = Σ_(j∈J) [E_j^sense + E_j^proc + E_j^tx] ≤ E_max,
[0059] G(J) is connected  (equivalently, λ_2(L_J) > 0 for an undirected communication graph).
[0060] Here ζ_j is sea-ice concentration at candidate j, I_c is the admissible ice threshold, E_max is the energy budget, G(J) is the communication graph induced by the selected nodes, and λ_2(L_J) is the algebraic connectivity of its graph Laplacian.
[0061] 3.4 Soft Operational Penalties
[0062] Maintenance cost and failure risk do not necessarily make a deployment impossible, but they reduce its desirability. They are therefore modeled as soft penalties:
[0063] P_soft(J) = λ_m C_maint(J) + λ_f R_fail(J).
[0064] This hard/soft separation is intentional: violating an ice, energy, node-budget, or connectivity requirement produces an infeasible solution, whereas a high-maintenance or high-risk solution remains feasible but is penalized.

[0230] 4.13 Convergence and Numerical Implementation
[0231] The variational objective is the evidence lower bound for the augmented model:
[0232] L(q)=𝔼_{q}[log p(y_{𝒥},0,Θ|x_{L})]-𝔼_{q}[log q(Θ)].(4-55)
[0233] The Gaussian coefficient factor follows by collecting the quadratic and linear terms in α in (4-20). The Gamma factors follow by collecting the log-precision and precision terms, and the Gaussian fidelity factor follows by collecting the quadratic terms in ρ. All Gamma distributions use shape–rate parameterization, with positive prior shapes and rates. The Student-t degrees of freedom ν > 0 is fixed before inference; τ⁻¹ in (4-13) denotes squared scale, not variance. For ν > 2, the conditional marginal noise variance is ν/[τ(ν − 2)].
[0234] Coordinate updates continue until relative ELBO change is below ε_VB or the iteration limit is reached. When computing ELBO, use E[log z] = digamma(a_z*) − log b_z* for every Gamma factor. A relative change criterion applied to all factor parameters is an alternative when ELBO is unavailable and is reported as such. Missing observations are removed from y_J and H_J rather than set to zero. Proper priors regularize the model, but sufficiently informative observations and a nondegenerate low-fidelity field are still needed to identify fidelity coupling reliably.
[0235] Use Cholesky solves for the coefficient mean and covariance rather than explicitly inverting the precision matrix. Cache Ψ^{T}Ψ and Ψ^{T}x_{L}. The full N × N field covariance need not be stored: its diagonal and trace can be evaluated from Ψ and Σ_{α}. Positive-definite numerical regularization, if needed, is recorded separately from probabilistic discrepancy and observation noise.

[0253] 5. Multi-Constrained Effective-Information-Ratio Sensor Deployment
[0254] The deployment layer combines historical spatial variability with current posterior uncertainty to select sensing locations sequentially. Hard constraints define the admissible search domain, whereas operational costs determine preferences within that domain. All statistics derived from the training ensemble are estimated without using held-out target observations.
[0255] 5.1 Structural Entropy Gain
[0256] Let X_c contain T centered training snapshots on the N-location grid, with T > 1. The empirical spatial covariance is
[0257] K = (1)/(T-1)X_{c}X_{c}^{T}.(5-1)
[0258] For the current selected set J, the unexplained structural variance at candidate j is
[0259] s_{H}(j|𝒥)=K_{jj}-K_{j𝒥}(K_{𝒥𝒥}+ε_{K}I)^{-1}K_{𝒥j}.(5-2)
[0260] Here ε_{K} > 0 stabilizes the covariance solve; for an empty selected set, s_H = K_jj. Small negative values caused by round-off are clipped to zero. A dimensionless structural entropy score is
[0261] Δ H(j|𝒥)=(1)/(2)log(1+(s_{H}(j|𝒥))/(σ_{H}^{2})).(5-3)
[0262] The reference variance σ_{H}^{2} > 0 is specified from training data or a measurement noise floor and remains fixed during a deployment run. This score is the Gaussian scalar information gain associated with the residual structural variance and reference noise. It is a training-covariance surrogate rather than the exact mutual information under the full Student-t posterior.
[0263] 5.2 Expected Posterior Uncertainty Reduction
[0264] The basis row for a prospective observation is
[0265] h_{j}^{T}=Ψ_{j,:}.(5-4)
[0266] Holding the current variational hyperparameters fixed, a Gaussian observation with planning variance r_j > 0 gives the rank-one covariance update
[0267] Σ_{α}^{(j)}=Σ_{α}-(Σ_{α}h_{j}h_{j}^{T}Σ_{α})/(r_{j}+h_{j}^{T}Σ_{α}h_{j}).(5-5)
[0268] For an unobserved candidate without additional reliability information, the default planning variance is
[0269] r_{j}=̅(τ)^{-1}.(5-6)
[0270] If a positive historical reliability weight ω_{j} is available, use
[0271] r_{j}=(̅(τ) ω_{j})^{-1}.(5-7)
[0272] These expressions use reciprocal effective precision as a Gaussian planning approximation; they are not the marginal Student-t noise variance. For variance matching when nu > 2 and a_{τ}^{*} > 1, an alternative is r_j = [nu/(nu-2)] b_{τ}^{*}/(a_{τ}^{*}-1), with an explicitly specified reliability adjustment. The chosen convention is fixed before comparison with baselines. No future residual or latent local precision is inferred before observing y_j.
[0273] The current and prospective total field uncertainties are
[0274] U_{tot}=tr(ΨΣ_{α}Ψ^{T}).(5-8)
[0275] U_{tot}^{(j)}=tr(ΨΣ_{α}^{(j)}Ψ^{T}).(5-9)
[0276] Their difference defines the marginal uncertainty reduction:
[0277] Δ U(j|𝒥)=U_{tot}-U_{tot}^{(j)}.(5-10)
[0278] Substituting (5-5) into (5-10) and using the cyclic trace identity gives
[0279] Δ U(j|𝒥)=(h_{j}^{T}Σ_{α}Ψ^{T}ΨΣ_{α}h_{j})/(r_{j}+h_{j}^{T}Σ_{α}h_{j}).(5-11)
[0280] Thus candidate evaluation does not require the future measurement value. For an orthonormal basis, the numerator reduces to h_{j}^{T}Σ_{α}^{2}h_{j}. Nonnegative reduction is guaranteed for the fixed-hyperparameter update with a positive semidefinite covariance. After actual measurement acquisition, full VB refitting can change the noise and discrepancy precisions, so monotonic reduction of the refitted covariance is not guaranteed.
[0281] 5.3 Redundancy and Score Normalization
[0282] To penalize duplicate historical spatial information, define the magnitude of pairwise correlation by
[0283] c_{jℓ}=(|K_{jℓ}|)/(root[]((K_{jj}+ε_{R})(K_{ℓℓ}+ε_{R}))).(5-12)
[0284] The positive regularizer ε_{R} has variance units. Correlations are clipped to [0,1] only for numerical round-off. Redundancy is the maximum correlation with any selected location:
[0285] Red(j,𝒥)=max_{ℓ∈𝒥}c_{jℓ}.(5-13)
[0286] For J empty, Red = 0. Absolute correlation treats both strongly correlated and strongly anticorrelated measurements as potentially redundant. Conditional entropy already discounts predictable structure; the additional redundancy coefficient controls any further preference for spatial diversity and is examined by ablation.
[0287] Because entropy and variance reduction have different units, normalize their raw values over the current feasible set F(J):
[0288] ̃(Δ H)_{j}=(Δ H_{j})/(max_{ℓ∈ℱ(𝒥)}Δ H_{ℓ}+ε_{H}).(5-14)
[0289] ̃(Δ U)_{j}=(Δ U_{j})/(max_{ℓ∈ℱ(𝒥)}Δ U_{ℓ}+ε_{U}).(5-15)
[0290] ̃(Red)_{j}=Red(j,𝒥).(5-16)
[0291] The positive stabilizers ε_{H} and ε_{U} have the units of their respective raw scores. If all gains vanish, their normalized values are zero. Max scaling preserves zero gain and avoids artificial gains when every candidate has the same raw value. Normalization is recomputed at each iteration after feasibility filtering; both raw and normalized quantities are retained for sensitivity analysis. Iteration-wise normalized scores do not define a single stationary set function or directly comparable cumulative information units.
[0292] 5.4 Effective Information and Soft Operational Penalties
[0293] The unnormalized information combination is
[0294] Δ I_{eff}^{raw}=Δ H+β_{raw}Δ U-η_{raw}Red.(5-17)
[0295] Its weights must supply the required unit conversions. The deployment score used here is the dimensionless normalized combination
[0296] Δ I_{eff}=̃(Δ H)+β̃(Δ U)-η̃(Red),  β,η≥0.(5-18)
[0297] For candidate j, maintenance burden and failure risk enter as soft penalties:
[0298] P_{soft,j}=λ_{m} C_{j}^{maint}+λ_{f} R_{j}^{fail}.(5-19)
[0299] C_{j}^{eff}=C_{j}^{base}+P_{soft,j}+ε_{0}.(5-20)
[0300] The costs are nonnegative and ε_{0} > 0 prevents a zero denominator. The weights λ_{m} and λ_{f} express maintenance and risk in the same cost units as the base deployment cost. In particular, a failure probability is converted to an expected burden rather than added to monetary cost without a conversion. The effective information ratio is
[0301] EIR(j|𝒥)=(Δ I_{eff}(j|𝒥))/(C_{j}^{eff}).(5-21)
[0302] A candidate can have negative effective information when redundancy dominates. Such candidates are not selected under a nonnegative stopping threshold. High cost or risk reduces preference among feasible nodes, whereas violation of any hard constraint excludes a node regardless of its score.
[0303] 5.5 Hard Feasibility Constraints
[0304] At each decision time, the selected sensing nodes must satisfy
[0305] ζ_{ℓ}≤ I_{c}  ∀ℓ∈𝒥.(5-22)
[0306] |𝒥|≤ p_{max}.(5-23)
[0307] E(𝒥)=∑_{ℓ∈𝒥}(E_{ℓ}^{sense}+E_{ℓ}^{proc}+E_{ℓ}^{tx})≤ E_{max}.(5-24)
[0308] The energy terms refer to a common specified mission horizon. If transmission energy depends on routes, E(J union {j}) is recomputed for the prospective topology, including changed costs for existing nodes. Current sea-ice values are checked for the whole prospective set. A change that makes an existing deployment infeasible requires operational reassessment before expansion.
[0309] For an undirected communication graph with adjacency A_J and degree matrix D_J,
[0310] L_{𝒥}=D_{𝒥}-A_{𝒥}.(5-25)
[0311] G(𝒥) is connected ⇔ λ_{2}(L_{𝒥})>0.(5-26)
[0312] The equivalence applies to graphs with at least two vertices; a singleton is connected by convention. In numerical use, a small positive tolerance replaces the strict spectral comparison, or connectivity is checked directly by graph traversal. Edges follow a declared communication model, such as an admissible link whenever distance is within a communication radius. When gateways or anchors are present, connectivity is evaluated on the graph augmented with those fixed vertices. Their energy and budget treatment is specified in advance, and sensing anchors count toward the sensor budget.
[0313] The prospective feasible set is
[0314] ℱ(𝒥)={j∈𝒱∖𝒥: 𝒥∪{j} satisfies (5-22)--(5-26)}.(5-27)
[0315] Here V is the candidate grid. Hard constraints are enforced before scoring and never appear as finite penalties in the EIR denominator. A connected feasible seed is supplied; the algorithm does not silently add infeasible relay nodes.
[0316] 5.6 Greedy Selection and Closed-Loop Updating
[0317] At iteration t, the next location is
[0318] j_{t}^{*}=argmax_{j∈ℱ(𝒥_{t})}EIR(j|𝒥_{t}).(5-28)
[0319] Ties are resolved using a fixed deterministic rule. Selection terminates if the node budget is reached, the feasible set is empty, or the largest marginal EIR fails to exceed a prescribed nonnegative threshold ε_{EIR}. After selection,
[0320] 𝒥_{t+1}=𝒥_{t}∪{j_{t}^{*}},  y_{𝒥_{t+1}}=[y_{𝒥_{t}}^{T},y_{j_{t}^{*}}]^{T}.(5-29)
[0321] The new measurement is physically acquired or supplied by the declared observation model in a simulation. RBMF-CS is rerun on the expanded dataset using previous variational parameters as initialization. A new local precision is initialized for the added measurement, then all variational factors are updated. The graph, energy state, covariance, redundancy, feasible set, and normalization are recomputed before the next decision. The rank-one update serves candidate planning; it does not replace robust assimilation of the acquired measurement.
[0322] For reporting, a cumulative ratio can be defined from raw component gains and their explicitly unit-adjusted weights:
[0323] EIR_{global}=(∑_{t}Δ I_{eff,t}^{raw})/(∑_{t} C_{j_{t}^{*}}^{eff}).(5-30)
[0324] This is a trajectory-level accounting metric, not a sum of marginal ratios or an exact joint mutual information. No approximation guarantee is claimed for greedy selection under coupled connectivity constraints, adaptive posterior refitting, and iteration-dependent normalization. A feasible intermediate node with low immediate score may be necessary to access a distant informative region, making the result dependent on the initial seed and stopping threshold.

[0331] 4:     Refresh environmental and operational states; reassess feasibility of the existing deployment.

[0333] 6:     if F(J) is empty then stop with reason “no feasible candidate”.
[0334] 7:     for each j in F(J) do

Any additional candidate screening is documented as a computational approximation and evaluated against the complete feasible search.
[0349] 6. Reconstruction–Deployment Closed Loop and Evaluation Protocol
[0350] 6.1 Closed-Loop Workflow
[0351] The proposed system alternates between probabilistic reconstruction and constrained deployment:
[0352] Construct multi-fidelity training data and learn or initialize the spatial basis Ψ.
[0353] Run RBMF-CS using the current sparse observations to obtain the posterior mean and uncertainty map.
[0354] Compute entropy gain, expected uncertainty reduction, and redundancy for feasible candidate nodes.
[0355] Filter candidates using sea-ice, node-budget, energy, and connectivity constraints.
[0356] Rank feasible candidates by EIR after maintenance-cost and failure-risk penalties.
[0357] Add the selected node, update observations and resource states, and repeat until termination.
[0358] 6.2 Data Splitting and Generalization Tests
[0359] Random train/test splitting alone can overestimate generalization when adjacent time windows or spatially correlated observations appear in both sets. The evaluation will therefore include temporal holdout, spatial holdout, and seasonal or regime-shift tests in addition to a conventional random split. This follows the deployment-aware evaluation principle that distribution shift should be tested explicitly rather than inferred from in-distribution accuracy [2].

[0403] 7.2 Posterior Calibration and Uncertainty Quality
[0404] The proposed method should be evaluated not only by point error but also by whether its uncertainty is statistically meaningful. Plot predicted uncertainty against empirical error, report 95% PICP and interval width, and compare calibration across normal and shifted conditions. A desirable result is not simply the narrowest interval; it is an interval that is both informative and correctly calibrated.

[0406] Noise and corruption experiments should progressively increase the outlier ratio, heavy-tailed noise level, missing-data rate, and systematic bias between low- and high-fidelity sources. The heavy-tailed RBMF-CS likelihood is expected to degrade more gracefully than Gaussian BCS. If performance deteriorates sharply beyond a threshold, that threshold should be reported as an operational boundary rather than omitted.

[0408] Compare the spatial distributions produced by Random, QR, QR-W, ice-constrained QR, uncertainty sampling, and MC-EIR. [0410] Plot cumulative entropy gain, cumulative uncertainty reduction, redundancy, and global EIR against the number of deployed nodes. [0411] 7.6 Ablation Results
[0412] The ablation study should identify which terms are responsible for each observed benefit. [0414] Report results separately for random split, temporal holdout, spatial holdout, and seasonal shift. [0417] 7.9 Overall Discussion
[0418] The expected contribution of the proposed framework is not that every metric is simultaneously optimized without trade-off. Rather, it changes the decision criterion from “select the most informative location” to “select the most valuable feasible location under uncertainty.” The posterior reconstruction layer supplies a principled estimate of where information is missing, while the deployment layer prevents the algorithm from recommending physically or operationally invalid locations. The resulting closed loop is therefore better aligned with real polar sensing than deterministic entropy maximization alone.
[0419] Several limitations should be discussed in the final paper. First, the multi-fidelity discrepancy model may be too simple when source relationships are nonlinear or strongly non-stationary. Second, energy and maintenance models may initially be simulation-based rather than derived from long-term field deployments. Third, strict connectivity can make greedy selection sensitive to the initial anchor set. Fourth, posterior uncertainty depends on model specification and should not be interpreted as absolute physical truth. These limitations motivate nonlinear multi-fidelity priors, adaptive communication models, and field validation in future work.
[0420] 8. Conclusions
[0421] This paper formulates polar sparse sensing as a coupled probabilistic reconstruction and engineering-feasible deployment problem. A robust Bayesian multi-fidelity compressed sensing model is proposed to fuse heterogeneous-fidelity observations, suppress the influence of non-Gaussian disturbances, and provide both reconstructed fields and posterior uncertainty. Based on this uncertainty, an effective information ratio combines entropy gain, uncertainty reduction, and redundancy suppression to quantify the marginal value of candidate sensing locations. Sea-ice concentration, node budget, energy, and communication connectivity are treated as hard constraints, whereas maintenance cost and failure risk are incorporated as soft penalties. The resulting reconstruction–deployment loop extends previous entropy-weighted sparse placement from deterministic information maximization to uncertainty-aware, multi-fidelity, constraint-aware sensing optimization. The final experimental study will evaluate not only reconstruction error but also probabilistic calibration, robustness, generalization under environmental shift, energy and communication cost, feasibility, and ablation effects. Future work will investigate nonlinear multi-fidelity relationships, dynamic redeployment under time-varying sea ice, adaptive communication topology, and validation using field-operated polar sensing platforms.
